Sector: HEMS / Heat Pump Technology Company size: Small–Medium Market: Germany

Scenario

Chleo runs a small-to-medium manufacturer that sells a smart heat pump bundled with an app-based HEMS (Home Energy Management System) layer, entering the German market. Two field problems recur: (1) installers hit fault codes they can't resolve on-site without escalating to senior technicians, and (2) the heat pump controller's connection to the HEMS app is unreliable, so customers report "broken" units that are actually just connectivity issues — inflating support cost and eroding trust in the product.

Why this problem, why now

  • Germany's political target is 500,000 heat pumps/year from 2024 (6M cumulative by 2030). Actual 2024 sales: only ~193,000 — down 46%, badly missing target; BWP forecasts a recovery to ~257,000 in 2025, still short. The gap is capacity-, not demand-driven.
  • Reaching the 2030 target independently requires a ~50% uplift in the certified-installer workforce (Aldersgate Group / EHPA) — a manufacturer cannot hire its way out of this; it has to make existing installers more effective per visit.
  • Root technical cause, industry-wide: inverter heat pumps run on proprietary, non-interoperable communication protocols (BACnet/Modbus/LonTalk), and rushed commissioning misses critical settings. US DOE-funded research puts this in perspective at the category level: 70–90% of AC/heat pump systems carry ≥1 install-related performance fault — a general HVAC figure, not Germany-specific, cited for scale.
  • Directly grounded in a real domain discussion — Octopus Energy / Vaillant aroTHERM users publicly struggling to tell a broken unit from a HEMS-connectivity fault — not a hypothetical scenario.

Data sources

Public: When2Heat Heating Profiles (Open Power System Data, CC BY 4.0) — real hourly German heat-pump COP/heat-demand data, 2007–2022. Self-authored synthetic: a 220-ticket fault-code → cause → fix-category dataset (seeded, reproducible generator), since no public dataset maps hardware-vs-connectivity directly — this gap is documented as an explicit assumption, not glossed over.

Note: the originally-scoped Kaggle "Heat Pump COP Drop – Synthetic Faults" dataset could not be fetched in this build environment (Kaggle requires authenticated access even for public datasets); When2Heat was substituted as a real, no-login equivalent covering the same COP domain.

Known risk / limitation — stated upfront

Guidance touching refrigerant/electrical commissioning steps sits near safety-critical territory — the copilot is framed as decision support with a human installer in the loop, never an autonomous fix authority.

No real customer, installer, or company data is used anywhere in Round 1; company name and figures are the assumed capstone scenario, and every dataset limitation above is disclosed rather than presented as production-grade.

Why an AI copilot, not a rule engine

The input side of this problem is messy, free-text field language — installer complaints and fault descriptions with no fixed schema, inconsistent phrasing, and mixed vocabulary between "the unit," "the app," and "the HEMS." That is exactly the terrain where natural-language understanding beats a hardcoded decision tree.
